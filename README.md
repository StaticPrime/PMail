# PMail

**Your inbox and your calendar together in one app, minus the ads and the snooping.**

PMail is an email, calendar and contacts app for the Mac. Add a Google, Microsoft or iCloud account, or any IMAP or POP mail account, and its mail, calendars and contacts are all in one window, a click apart. Add as many accounts as you like: switch from the picture in the corner and all three switch with you. Read whole conversations at a glance, write with formatting, take back a message you've just sent, archive with one key, plan your week with a Meet, Teams or FaceTime meeting on any event, and keep everyone you write to close at hand.

- Mail, Calendar and Contacts side by side in one window
- Any number of accounts, each with its own mail, calendars and contacts
- Conversations: a message and its replies together, your own replies included
- Inbox tabs that sort mail into Primary, Promotions, Social and Updates (Google accounts) or Focused and Other (Microsoft accounts)
- Undo for archiving, deleting, spam and moving, and Undo Send
- A junk mail filter that learns from what you report, on top of your provider's own
- New mail as it arrives, with notifications, and everything you've opened still readable offline
- Snooze conversations until later, schedule messages to send later, and an Outbox for mail written offline
- A video meeting on any event in one click: Meet on Google calendars, Teams on Microsoft calendars, and FaceTime links on iCloud calendars
- Day, week, month and schedule views, with every calendar in its own color
- Events, tasks, out of office and focus time, with your Google Tasks and Microsoft To Do lists beside your calendars
- Repeating events, several notifications, private events and guest permissions, and Find a time to see when your guests are free
- Pictures in messages stay hidden until you ask, or load privately as mail arrives, so senders can't tell when you read their mail, and with Tor built in, where you are
- Contacts with labels and stars, and one-click buttons to email, invite, call or FaceTime someone

Works with: Google accounts (mail, calendars and contacts), Microsoft accounts (Outlook.com and Microsoft 365 mail, calendars and contacts), iCloud (Mail, Calendar and Contacts), and any mail server that speaks IMAP or POP with SMTP, with calendar (CalDAV) and contacts (CardDAV) servers if it has them.

PMail is free, needs no account of its own and has no tracking: it talks only to your own mail, calendar and contacts servers, and to Apple Maps for place suggestions while you type an event's location (which you can turn off). It runs in the macOS app sandbox, on macOS 14 Sonoma or later. It's written in Swift with AppKit and SwiftUI, and is part of the PuggleSoft family with [PuggleDocs](https://github.com/StaticPrime/PuggleDocs), [PuggleSheets](https://github.com/StaticPrime/PuggleSheets), [PuggleSlides](https://github.com/StaticPrime/PuggleSlides) and [PugglePhotos](https://github.com/StaticPrime/PugglePhotos).

## Features

### Accounts

- **Adding an account.** File ▸ Add Account… (or the welcome screen) offers Google, Microsoft, iCloud and Other mail account. Google and Microsoft accounts sign in on the provider's own page, in the system's secure sign-in window: PMail never sees the password, only a key the provider gives it, which you can take back at any time in your account's security settings. iCloud takes an app-specific password, made at account.apple.com (iCloud doesn't let other apps sign in through a web page); one password opens iCloud Mail, Calendar and Contacts. Any other account takes its address and password, with its servers filled in from the address (IMAP or POP for incoming mail, SMTP for outgoing, with SSL/TLS or STARTTLS) and optional calendar and contacts servers. Every account is checked against its server before it's added.
- **Switching.** Click the picture at the top right for the account switcher: the account showing, every other account to switch to, Add another account and Account settings. The Account menu lists them too, with ⌃⌘1 to ⌃⌘9. Switching changes mail, calendars and contacts together, and each account remembers where you were. The switcher shows how much unread mail each account's inbox has, and the Dock icon shows them all added up.
- **Account settings.** The name on mail you send, a signature added under new messages, the account's servers, signing in again, and removing the account (nothing is deleted from the server).
- **Secrets.** Passwords and sign-in keys are kept in your Mac's keychain, never in PMail's own files.

### Mail

- **The window.** The rail on the far left moves between Mail, Calendar and Contacts (⌘1, ⌘2, ⌘3). Mail's sidebar has Compose, then Inbox, Starred, Sent and Drafts (and Snoozed and Important where the account has them), More for Spam, Trash, Archive and All Mail, then the account's own folders and its labels, nested, each with its unread count. The big search field runs across the top. The menu button at the top left (or ⌃⌘S) narrows the sidebar to icons. Light and Dark Mode both work.
- **The list.** A row for each conversation: tick box, star, who wrote (or, in Sent, who it went to), the subject and the start of the latest message, a paper clip for attachments, and when. Unread conversations are bold. Point at a row for Archive, Delete and Mark as read in place of its date; right-click it for more. Fifty conversations to a page, with "1–50 of 2,345" and arrows for older and newer pages.
- **Inbox categories.** The inbox shows in tabs, each with its count of unread messages so nothing seems missing: Google accounts' own Primary, Promotions, Social and Updates, Microsoft accounts' Focused and Other, and for every other account (iCloud, IMAP, POP) Primary, Transactions, Updates, Promotions and Social, sorted on your Mac from the sender and the message: list and newsletter headers, receipts and shipping notices, sign-in codes, social networks. Mail from people stays in Primary. Move a conversation to another category (right-click, the toolbar, or drag it onto a tab) and that sender's mail goes there from now on; Settings ▸ Accounts lists those senders. Turn categories off in Settings ▸ Mail, or Show Categories in the list's ⋯ menu, and every inbox is one list.
- **Labels.** Every account can label conversations, several labels each, beside its folders. Google accounts use their own labels and Microsoft accounts their categories; an IMAP account keeps labels on the server as keywords where it allows them, and on your Mac where it doesn't (an iCloud account, for one), and a POP account on your Mac. Make one with the + beside Labels in the sidebar (or New Label… wherever labels are offered), edit or delete it by right-clicking it, put labels on and take them off from the Labels button, the right-click menu, or by dragging a conversation onto a label. A label lists every conversation with it, and labels show as colored tags on rows and conversations.
- **Ticking rows.** Tick rows, or use the select menu for all, none, read, unread, starred or unstarred; the toolbar then archives, reports spam, deletes, marks read or unread, moves and labels them all at once. Drag rows onto a folder or label in the sidebar.
- **Reading.** Click a conversation to open it: its subject and labels, then each message, the newest open and older ones folded to a line (click to open them). Each message shows who sent it, to whom ("to me, Alex" opens the full From, To, Cc and date), when ("2 hours ago"), its star, Reply, and more: Reply all, Forward, Delete this message, Mark unread from here, Download message and Unsubscribe. Reply, Reply all and Forward wait at the bottom. The arrows at the top go to newer and older conversations; ← goes back to the list. In Dark Mode, formatted messages sit on a white card, as their senders drew them.
- **Pictures and privacy.** Pictures from the internet in a message can tell the sender when and where it was read, so Settings ▸ Privacy decides how they load. Block all remote content (on at first) keeps them hidden until you ask: Show pictures loads them for that message, and Always show pictures from the sender remembers (the Privacy pane lists those senders). Protect mail activity instead loads every new message's pictures in the background as it arrives, whether you open it or not, so senders see them fetched as for everyone and can't tell whether or when you read their mail; opening a message shows PMail's copy. With both off, pictures load as you open a message. Whatever loads goes through PMail's own loader, with no cookies and nothing that says which app or Mac asked, and never from your Mac or your local network. To hide your IP address too, turn on Hide my IP address from senders. Pictures then load through Tor, which is built into PMail, starts only while this is on and needs nothing set up: the Privacy pane shows it connecting. Or choose a SOCKS5 or HTTP proxy of your own, with a user name and password if it needs them; Test Proxy checks that it answers without loading anything. While it's on nothing loads directly: until Tor or the proxy can be reached, pictures wait, and if they can't be reached, pictures don't load. Pictures sent inside a message always show, a message's own scripts never run, and links open in your browser.
- **Attachments.** Files show as cards under their message: click to save to Downloads, double-click to open, or right-click for Save As….
- **Actions with Undo.** Archive, Delete, Report spam and Move leave the list at once, and a note along the bottom offers Undo for a few seconds before anything reaches the server. Star and Mark as read or unread happen right away. Opening a conversation marks it read.
- **Sent, Drafts, Archive, Junk and Trash on every account.** When a server has no folder for one of them (an iCloud account that has only ever had an inbox, for one, or any POP account), PMail keeps that folder on your Mac: sent copies and drafts are saved there, and archived, junk and deleted mail is copied there before it leaves the server. Move to Inbox (or Not spam) puts a message back on the server, and Delete forever in that Trash removes it for good. If the server gets its own folder later, the one on your Mac shows as “Sent on this Mac” (and so on) until it's empty. The spam folder is called Spam or Junk, as the server words it, and Junk when it says neither or PMail keeps it.
- **Junk mail.** PMail has a junk filter of its own, on top of your provider's. It learns what junk looks like from the mail you report as spam and the mail you mark Not Junk, starting from each account's inbox and spam folder, and judges new mail as it arrives. In Settings ▸ Junk Mail you choose what happens to junk: marked and left in the inbox (with a Junk mark in the list, and a note on the message with Not Junk and Move to Junk), moved to the account's Junk or Spam folder, or whatever you set up under Advanced. Mail from people in your contacts, people you've written to, and mail addressed to you by your full name is never junk, unless you say otherwise. PMail can also trust the junk marks some servers' spam filters put in a message's headers. Advanced is a rule: if all (or any) of its conditions are met (the sender, recipients, subject, text, dates, account, whether the sender is in your contacts, starred, has a contact label or is someone you've written to, whether the message is junk, signed or encrypted, its priority, attachments or any header), it does its actions (move it to Junk, the Trash or the archive, mark it as junk, label it, mark it read, star it, notify you, play a sound or bounce PMail's icon in the Dock). Junk never loads its pictures by itself. Reset forgets what the filter learned.
- **Blocking senders.** Report spam asks whether to block the sender too: their address, or everything from their domain. Their other conversations in the inbox go to the spam folder with it, and their new mail is dealt with as it arrives, without a notification: moved to the Junk or Spam folder at first, or, as Settings ▸ Junk Mail ▸ Blocked says, marked and left in the inbox (with Unblock on the message), or moved to the Trash. Undo right after reporting unblocks them again; later, Settings ▸ Accounts lists everyone blocked on each account, with Unblock.
- **Search.** Type in the search field and press Return. Google and Microsoft accounts search all your mail in their own search language; IMAP accounts search the folder showing; POP accounts search the mail on your Mac.

### Writing

- **Compose.** Compose (or ⌘N, or C) opens a message in a window docked at the bottom right of the main window, up to three side by side. Minimize it to its title, make it bigger, or close it to keep a draft.
- **Drafts.** A draft opens straight into its window to carry on writing, from Drafts or from the conversation it replies to, where it shows as Draft with Edit and Discard. Opening it again brings that window forward; saving replaces the draft, and sending or discarding deletes it. Drafts aren't mail yet, so there's nothing to reply to, mark read, star or file away: ticking drafts offers only Discard Drafts (with Undo).
- **Addresses.** Type a name or address in To, Cc or Bcc and PMail suggests people from your contacts; Return, Tab or a comma turns what you typed into a chip, and Delete takes the last one back. Paste a whole list at once.
- **Formatting.** The Aa button shows the formatting bar: the font (Sans Serif, Serif, Fixed Width, Wide, Narrow and a handful of familiar faces), four sizes, bold, italic and underline (⌘B, ⌘I, ⌘U), text and highlight colors from a palette, alignment, numbered and bulleted lists, indenting, quotes, strikethrough, and Remove formatting. Links (⌘K), emoji and your signature go in from the buttons along the bottom, and pictures go into the text where the cursor is. Every message goes out as both formatted and plain text, with fonts named the way other mail apps understand.
- **Signatures.** Each account's signature can have fonts, colors, links and pictures, written with the same formatting bar (in the account's settings, from the account menu or Settings ▸ Accounts). New messages start with it, and the signature button puts it in where the cursor is. A Google account's signature is the one on Google: PMail reads it when the account opens and when its settings do, and saves changes there, so it matches the web; pictures in it come from the web, as Google keeps them. Other accounts' signatures are PMail's own, kept on this Mac with their pictures inside.
- **More options.** The ⋮ button switches a message to plain text (taking the formatting out, after asking), prints it, turns spell checking on or off, opens new messages full size from now on, and puts labels on the message for when it's sent.
- **Files.** Attach files with the paper clip, or drag them onto the message.
- **Replies and forwards.** Replies go to the right people (Reply all leaves you out and keeps everyone else; replying to your own message goes to the people it went to), with "Re:" and the original quoted underneath (folded away; click ••• to see it), and stay in their conversation. Forwards bring the original's attachments.
- **Sending.** Send (or ⌘Return) closes the window and sends a few seconds later; Undo in the note along the bottom stops it and opens it again. Settings sets how long the wait is (5 to 30 seconds).
- **Links from other apps.** PMail opens `mailto:` links as new messages, so it can be your Mac's email app.
- **Settings.** PMail ▸ Settings (⌘,) has six panes: General (new mail, opening at login, the keyboard), Mail (Undo Send, inbox categories), Junk Mail (junk and blocked senders' mail), Privacy (pictures and other remote content: protecting mail activity, blocking, and hiding your IP address through Tor or a proxy of your own), Accounts (for the account picked: the settings its account menu also offers, so the name on sent mail, the signature, its servers, signing in again and removing it, then its own blocked senders and the senders put in a category; every account keeps its own) and Signing In.

### Keeping up to date

- **New mail.** PMail checks every account's inbox every couple of minutes while it's open (Settings sets how often; accounts other than the one showing are checked half as often). An IMAP account (iCloud included) is watched all the time while it's showing: new mail appears the moment the server says so, or within a minute on servers that can't, and mail read, moved or deleted on another device updates too. The list showing and the unread counts update by themselves.
- **Calendars and contacts.** Each mail check (and Refresh in Mail, ⌘R) also gets the account's latest calendars, events, tasks and contacts, in the background, so events and contacts added on your phone or another computer show up without a refresh and without holding up the mail.
- **Notifications.** New mail shows a notification with who it's from, the subject and the start of the message, unless you're looking at that inbox already; click it to open the conversation, switching accounts if need be. Settings turns them off.
- **Offline.** Folders, the lists you've looked at, conversations you've opened, calendars and contacts are kept on your Mac, so PMail opens straight away and you can read them without a network. A note across the top says when you're offline. What you do offline waits and reaches the server once it can be reached: messages you send go to the Outbox; drafts are kept on the Mac (at the top of Drafts, where you can open them to carry on writing) and go to the server's Drafts folder; events, tasks and contacts you make, change or delete show at once, with a note that they're waiting, and go to their calendar, task list or contacts; and archiving, moving, labeling and marking mail is done when the server answers. Drafts, the Outbox and calendar and contact changes are kept in the account's folder, so they wait even if PMail quits.
- **The Outbox.** A message sent while offline (or when the server can't be reached) waits in the Outbox, which appears in the sidebar while it has something in it, and goes as soon as it can. Open it there to Send Now, Edit or Delete it.
- **Snooze.** The clock button (on the toolbar, when you point at a row, or in the right-click menu) puts conversations away until later today, tomorrow, this weekend, next week, or a date and time you pick. They leave the inbox at once (with Undo) and come back to the top, unread, with a notification, when their time comes. Snoozed lists them with when they'll be back; Unsnooze brings one back now.
- **Scheduled send.** The arrow beside Send schedules the message for tomorrow morning or afternoon, Monday morning, or a date and time you pick. Scheduled lists what's waiting, with when it will go; open one to Send Now, Cancel Send (to edit it again) or Delete it.
- **Open at login.** Settings ▸ General ▸ Open PMail when I log in, so new mail, snoozes and scheduled messages keep coming.

### Calendar

- **The window.** Create at the top of the sidebar (an event, a task, out of office or focus time), a small month to jump to any day, then My calendars, Tasks and Other calendars, each with a colored box to show or hide it. Today, the arrows and the month's name across the top, with the view on the right.
- **Day and week.** A column for each day, the hours down the side, the time zone in the corner, all-day and multi-day events as bars across the top, and a red line at the time now. Events that overlap share the column. Click an empty slot to make an event there, or a day's number to see just that day.
- **Month.** Whole weeks, each day with as many events as fit (all-day ones as bars, the rest with a colored dot and their time), and "3 more" for the rest.
- **Schedule.** The next thirty days as a list, a day at a time. Searching the calendar lists every event that matches, by title, place, description or guest.
- **Event details.** Click an event for its time (and how it repeats), its kind, the meeting to join, its guests and their answers, where it is (click for Maps), its description, reminder and calendar, with Edit, Delete and Email guests at the top, and Going? Yes, No or Maybe for invitations.
- **Making and changing events.** Its kind, title, when (or all day) and how it repeats, the video meeting, place (with places and addresses from Apple Maps suggested as you type: click one, or pick it with the arrow keys and Return), notifications, calendar, color, busy or free, visibility, description (with bold, italic, underline, numbered and bulleted lists, and links, from the bar above it or ⌘B, ⌘I, ⌘U and ⌘K), and guests with suggestions from your contacts and what they may do; guests get invitations by email from your provider when you save. Deleting an event asks first, since it can't be undone; a repeating event asks whether a change or deletion is for that event or all of them.
- **Repeating.** Beside All day: Does not repeat, Daily, Weekly on its weekday, Monthly on its weekday of the month (the fourth Sunday, say), Annually on its date, Every weekday, or Custom…: every so many days, weeks (on the weekdays you pick), months (on the same day, or the same weekday of the month) or years, never ending, ending on a day, or after so many times. Event details say how an event repeats in words. A new rule is for the whole series.
- **Notifications.** As many as the calendar keeps, each so many minutes, hours, days or weeks before: five on Google calendars, one on Microsoft calendars (which keep only one), and several on iCloud and other calendars.
- **Visibility and guest permissions.** Default visibility, Public or Private, beside Busy and Free, where the calendar has them (Google calendars; Default or Private on Microsoft calendars; CalDAV servers other than iCloud). Under the guests, whether they may modify the event, invite others and see the guest list (all three on Google calendars, see the guest list on Microsoft calendars).
- **Find a time.** A tab beside Event details: a week (or a day, a column per person) of your own events and each guest's busy times in their own color, with the event's time over them; click a free half hour to move the event there. Suggested times, beside Guests, lists times in the next two weeks when everyone is free, on weekdays from 9 to 5. Guests' busy times come from your provider where it can look them up: on Google, people in your organization or who share their free/busy; on Microsoft, work and school accounts. Anyone whose busy times aren't available is named.
- **Kinds of events.** An event, a task, out of office or focus time, picked at the top of the editor. Out of office takes the whole day to start with and focus time a couple of hours; neither has guests or a video meeting, and both show their symbol on the calendar. A Google account's main calendar keeps out of office and focus time itself where the account is a work or school one, and can decline meetings during that time, with a message; a Microsoft calendar keeps out of office. Where a calendar can't keep a kind, it's saved as an ordinary event and PMail shows it as that kind on this Mac, so you can mark an iCloud event as focus time, say. An event without guests can change kind later; a meeting stays a meeting.
- **Tasks.** A Google account's task lists (Google Tasks) and a Microsoft account's lists (Microsoft To Do) show under Tasks, each task at the top of the day it's due. Click its circle to tick it off (it's struck through), or open it for its notes, Mark as done, Edit and Delete. New tasks go in the account's first list. iCloud and other accounts keep their tasks on this Mac, in a list called Tasks.
- **Keys.** D, W, M and A pick the view, T goes to today, J and K step forward and back, and C makes an event.

### Video meetings

- **Google calendars.** Add Google Meet video conferencing makes a Meet meeting for the event when you save; its link, meeting code and dial-in numbers go to every guest.
- **Microsoft calendars.** Add Microsoft Teams meeting makes a Teams meeting for the event when you save (on accounts where the calendar allows Teams meetings).
- **iCloud and other calendars.** Add FaceTime link: make a link in FaceTime (Create Link, then Copy Link) and paste it; PMail offers the one already copied. Guests join from a Mac, iPhone or iPad, or a web browser. Message guests opens Messages with every guest.
- **Joining.** Every event with a meeting, whichever app it's for (Meet, Teams, FaceTime, Zoom or Webex), has a Join button in its details.

### Contacts

- **The list.** Contacts, Other contacts (people your Google account remembers you writing to) and your labels in the sidebar; a table of names, email addresses, phone numbers, job titles and companies, and labels, with starred contacts first. Point at a row to star, edit or delete it. Searching filters as you type.
- **A contact's page.** A big picture, their name, job and labels, buttons to email them, invite them to an event, call them or FaceTime them, and every detail they have.
- **Editing.** Names (with prefix, middle name, suffix and nickname), company and job title, email addresses, phone numbers and postal addresses with labels, birthday, notes and labels.

### Your privacy

- **Nothing in between.** PMail connects straight to your own providers' servers; there's no PuggleSoft server and no PuggleSoft account. Nothing about you or your mail is sent anywhere else. The one exception is that while you type an event's location, what you've typed goes to Apple Maps to suggest places; Settings ▸ Privacy ▸ Suggest places from Apple Maps turns that off. (With Hide IP address on, pictures in messages go through Tor or your proxy instead of straight to the sender.)
- **No tracking.** No analytics, no ads, no crash reporting that calls home. Pictures that could tell senders when you read their mail stay hidden until you ask, or load privately before you open it (see Pictures and privacy).
- **Kept on your Mac.** The list of accounts is kept in PMail's own folder, and passwords and sign-in keys (and a proxy's password) in your keychain, the built-in Tor keeps what it learns about the Tor network in PMail's folder, and so does the junk filter what it learns about junk. So is a copy of what each account last loaded (folders, the lists and conversations you've opened, calendars and contacts), its snoozes, the senders it blocks, the people it has written to, its junk marks, the pictures its mail loaded from the internet and the messages waiting to be sent; removing an account removes them. So are the kinds of events its calendars can't keep (an iCloud event shown as focus time), and drafts, events, tasks and contacts saved while offline, until they're sent. Accounts without calendar or contacts servers keep their calendar and contacts in PMail's folder too, iCloud and other accounts their tasks, POP accounts their mail, and IMAP accounts whose server lacks them their Sent, Drafts, Archive, Junk and Trash.

Help ▸ Keyboard Shortcuts (⌘/) lists every keyboard shortcut. Single-key shortcuts (C to compose, E to archive, # to delete, R, A and F to reply, reply all and forward, S to star, J and K to move through conversations, U to go back to the list, / to search) work whenever you aren't typing, and can be turned off in Settings.

## Known limits

- Signing in with Google or Microsoft needs PMail's own client ID from each provider, built into the copy you run (see Signing in with Google and Microsoft below). A copy without them says so and still works with iCloud and other accounts; a Google account can then be added for mail only, with an app password, as an Other mail account.
- iCloud doesn't let other apps sign in through a web page, so iCloud accounts take an app-specific password.
- macOS gives apps no way to make FaceTime links, so a FaceTime link is made in FaceTime and pasted into the event.
- Teams meetings can only be added on Microsoft calendars that allow them (work and school accounts); personal accounts may not.
- Snoozes and scheduled messages are kept on this Mac, not on the server: they happen while PMail is open (or the next time it opens), and other apps and devices don't know about them.
- Archiving, moving, labeling and marking mail offline wait while PMail is open; if it quits before the server can be reached again, they're tried once more as it quits and otherwise lost. Drafts, the Outbox, and event, task and contact changes are kept until they're sent.
- An event made offline gets its Meet or Teams meeting when it reaches the calendar, and a repeating one shows only its first time until then. A waiting change the server turns down when it's sent (because the event or contact was changed or deleted elsewhere in the meantime, say) is dropped, and PMail says so.
- Folders kept on your Mac aren't on the server, so other devices and apps don't see them; searching shows what's in them only while that folder is showing.
- Blocking happens in PMail, not on the server: mail from a blocked sender arrives in the inbox as usual and goes to the spam folder when PMail next checks, so other devices can see it in the inbox until then. The list of blocked senders is kept on this Mac.
- PMail's junk filter runs in PMail too, on mail as PMail finds it, so junk it moves can show in the inbox on other devices until then. What it learned, the junk and blocked marks and the people you've written to are kept on this Mac. It judges a message by its sender, subject and first lines, and only once it has learned from 20 junk and 20 good messages. It doesn't judge the mail an account already had when it was added.
- Advanced holds one rule, for junk; PMail has no general mail rules yet, and no actions that reply, forward or run scripts.
- Microsoft and Google accounts hear about new mail by checking every couple of minutes; only IMAP servers can tell PMail at once. Microsoft accounts go by when each message arrived, so one held up on its way into the inbox (while an attachment is scanned, say) shows in the inbox without a notification.
- Out of office and focus time are kept by the calendar itself only on a Google work or school account's main calendar (and out of office on Microsoft calendars); elsewhere they're ordinary events to other apps and devices, and the kind is kept on this Mac. A repeating event's kind is kept for the whole series.
- Tasks show on the day they're due: tasks without a due date don't show, and a task's time of day isn't kept (Google keeps only the date). Tasks can't be moved to another list, and subtasks show as ordinary tasks. Accounts added before PMail showed tasks need to sign in again to allow them; the calendar's sidebar says so and has a Sign In Again button.
- Repeating events can be changed or deleted one at a time or all together, except that changing all of them leaves their time as it was; there's no "this and following events". The editor shows the rules it can make; an event that repeats some other way (on several days of the month, say) keeps its rule unless another is chosen.
- A description's formatting is bold, italic, underline, lists and links. Google and Microsoft calendars show it; iCloud and other calendars keep a formatted copy beside the plain text, and Apple's Calendar app shows the plain text. Fonts, colors and pictures in descriptions from other apps show as plain text, and tasks' notes are plain text.
- iCloud and other calendars can't look up other people's busy times, so Find a time and Suggested times show only your own events there. Microsoft personal accounts can't either. Suggested times are on weekdays from 9 to 5, in the Mac's time zone.
- Folders can't be made, renamed or deleted in PMail yet (labels can).
- Labels on an IMAP account are keywords other mail apps don't show; where a server doesn't take keywords they're kept on this Mac. Either way their colors live on this Mac, and deleting or renaming one doesn't reach PMail on another Mac.
- Microsoft doesn't let other apps read or change Outlook signatures, so a Microsoft account's signature is PMail's own, kept on this Mac, and doesn't match Outlook's.
- A Google signature's pictures have to come from the web (Google keeps them by their address); pictures from a file can go in other accounts' signatures.
- Microsoft doesn't let apps rename a category, so renaming one makes a new category and moves its messages there; calendar events and contacts keep the old one.
- Inbox categories for iCloud, IMAP and POP accounts are sorted on this Mac, in English. An IMAP inbox's newest 2,000 messages are sorted; older ones show under Primary. Other devices don't see these categories.
- POP accounts leave mail on the server, download the newest 300 messages the first time, and need the server to show the inbox.
- Without Hide IP address, Protect mail activity loads pictures from your own internet address, so senders can't tell whether or when you read their mail but can see roughly where you are. Tor or the proxy carries remote content only; PMail reaches your mail, calendar and contacts servers, and Apple Maps, directly.
- Through Tor, pictures load more slowly and a few image hosts refuse them. Where Tor itself is blocked it can't connect, since PMail doesn't offer bridges yet. The built-in Tor is the one PMail shipped with, updated with PMail's own releases.
- Protect mail activity loads pictures as PMail finds new mail. Messages that arrived before it was turned on, and messages over 5 MB, load their pictures when opened, and a picture that changes (a countdown, say) shows as it was when the message arrived. Video and sound from the internet never load.
- Contact pictures show for Google contacts but can't be changed in PMail; Microsoft contacts show without pictures.
- An IMAP conversation opened from the inbox includes your replies from Sent, but one opened from Sent doesn't bring in the inbox side.

## Building and running

Open `PMail.xcodeproj` in Xcode and run the **PMail** scheme. Or, from the command line:

```sh
xcodebuild -project PMail.xcodeproj -scheme PMail -configuration Debug \
  -derivedDataPath build/DerivedData build
open build/DerivedData/Build/Products/Debug/PMail.app
```

The app is sandboxed, with access to the network (to reach your servers), to listen on the Mac itself (for the built-in Tor), to files you choose (attachments) and to the Downloads folder (saving attachments). The build copies the Tor client from `Vendor/Tor` into the app and signs it with the app. Debug builds are signed to run on the Mac that built them, unless `Config/Signing.xcconfig` names a team; releases are signed by `Tools/make-installer.sh`.

To try PMail without signing in to anything, run a debug build with `-PMSampleAccounts YES`: three made-up accounts (one of each provider kind), with mail, calendars and contacts that change in memory only.

### Signing in with Google and Microsoft

Each provider asks apps that sign in through its page to be registered with it. Register PMail once with each, then give PMail the client IDs.

**Google.** In the Google Cloud console, make a project and enable the Gmail API, the Google Calendar API, the Google Tasks API and the People API. Set up the OAuth consent screen (External) with the scopes `https://mail.google.com/`, `https://www.googleapis.com/auth/gmail.settings.basic` (for the signature), `https://www.googleapis.com/auth/calendar`, `https://www.googleapis.com/auth/tasks`, `https://www.googleapis.com/auth/contacts` and `https://www.googleapis.com/auth/contacts.other.readonly`, and add yourself as a test user. Then make an OAuth client ID of the type **iOS**, with the bundle ID `com.pugglesoft.PMail`. Its client ID (`…apps.googleusercontent.com`) is `PMAIL_GOOGLE_CLIENT_ID`; PMail works out the redirect URI from it (the client ID reversed, `com.googleusercontent.apps.…:/oauth2redirect`). Until Google verifies the app, only the test users on the consent screen can sign in; full mail access is a restricted scope, which Google reviews before anyone else can use it.

**Microsoft.** In the Microsoft Entra admin center, under App registrations, make a new registration named PMail for accounts in any organizational directory and personal Microsoft accounts. Add the platform **iOS / macOS** with the bundle ID `com.pugglesoft.PMail` (which gives the redirect URI `msauth.com.pugglesoft.PMail://auth`), and turn on Allow public client flows. Under API permissions, add the Microsoft Graph delegated permissions `User.Read`, `Mail.ReadWrite`, `Mail.Send`, `MailboxSettings.ReadWrite` (for Outlook categories, which PMail shows as labels), `Calendars.ReadWrite`, `Tasks.ReadWrite` (for Microsoft To Do), `Contacts.ReadWrite` and `offline_access`. Its Application (client) ID is `PMAIL_MICROSOFT_CLIENT_ID`.

Then give PMail the IDs, in any of these ways:

- **In a file, for every build on this Mac:** put them in `Config/Secrets.xcconfig`, which Git ignores:

  ```
  PMAIL_GOOGLE_CLIENT_ID = 1234567890-abcdefg.apps.googleusercontent.com
  PMAIL_MICROSOFT_CLIENT_ID = 00000000-0000-0000-0000-000000000000
  ```

- **As environment variables:** in the shell that runs `xcodebuild` or `Tools/make-installer.sh`, or in Xcode's scheme under Run ▸ Arguments ▸ Environment Variables (PMail reads those when it starts).
- **For releases:** as repository secrets (see Releasing a new version).

| Setting | What it holds |
| --- | --- |
| `PMAIL_GOOGLE_CLIENT_ID` | The OAuth client ID registered for PMail with Google |
| `PMAIL_GOOGLE_CLIENT_SECRET` | Optional: the client's secret, for a registration type that has one |

### Installer

```sh

```

This builds `build/PMail.pkg`, which installs PMail.app in /Applications for all users (macOS asks for an administrator's password). The app runs on every Mac with macOS 14 or later. ### Updating Tor

`Vendor/Tor` holds the Tor client PMail ships, from the Tor Project's Tor Expert Bundle for macOS, so building never downloads anything. When the Tor Project releases a new version (security fixes most of all), set `TOR_BUNDLE_VERSION` at the top of `Tools/update-tor.sh`, run `Tools/update-tor.sh --pin` and put the sums it prints in the script, run `Tools/update-tor.sh` again, then commit `Vendor/Tor` and release PMail. The script checks every download against the sums pinned in it and the release's own list of sums, and with gpg installed, that list's signature too.

### Reading a crash report

Release builds are stripped, so a crash report from an installed copy shows the PMail lines of its stack only as addresses. Every release carries `PMail.dSYM.zip`, the debug symbols for that build. Unzip the one for the version in the report and look the addresses up, giving the load address from the PMail line under Binary Images:


## How it works

- **One account at a time, all of it.** Each account has a session: its mail, calendar and contacts services, made from its kind and its keychain secret, and what the three parts of the window show for it. Switching accounts swaps the whole session, so mail, calendars and contacts always belong together, and each keeps its place.
- **One interface, many servers.** Mail, calendars and contacts each go through one interface, with an implementation for each kind of server: IMAP or POP with SMTP, the Google and Microsoft web APIs, CalDAV and CardDAV, or files on the Mac. Lists come a page of conversations at a time, so nothing waits for a whole mailbox.
- **Mail protocols.** IMAP, POP and SMTP run over URLSession stream tasks, with TLS from the first byte or STARTTLS before signing in, and refuse to send a password over a plain connection unless the account says so. Whole messages are read by PMail's own MIME parser, whatever server they come from, and every message sent is written by its MIME builder: plain text and HTML, attachments and inline pictures, encoded headers, and never a Bcc header.
- **Sign-in.** OAuth 2.0 with PKCE in the system's sign-in window, in a private session so nothing stays in a browser. The refresh token is kept in the keychain; access tokens are refreshed one at a time as they run out, and once more when a server turns one down.
- **Undo.** Archiving, deleting, spam and moves change the list at once and reach the server a few seconds later; Undo cancels the wait. Sending waits the same way. Anything still waiting is sent before PMail quits or switches accounts.
- **Message pages.** Each message body is drawn by WebKit at its full height inside the conversation. The page's scripts are off, and a content security policy and a content blocker keep it from reaching the internet at all: the pictures, style sheets and fonts it's allowed are pointed at PMail's own `pmail-remote:` addresses, which PMail answers from its copy or its private loader. Pictures inside the message come from the message itself, links aren't looked up ahead of a click, and every link goes to the browser.
- **Remote content.** PMail's loader asks for pictures, style sheets and fonts in a session that keeps and sends nothing of its own (no cookies, cache or saved passwords), with the browser name every Mac's WebKit gives and the same languages for everyone, and refuses addresses on the Mac or its local network, redirects included. Style sheets come back with their own addresses pointed at the loader. What it loads is kept in each account's folder, a server's refusal included, so each address is asked for once; copies go after half a year, or the oldest once an account's pass 512 MB. With mail activity protected, each check for new mail loads the new messages (up to 5 MB each) and their remote content in the background. A proxy goes into the session's proxy settings with fallback off, so a proxy that can't be reached means nothing loads, and a SOCKS5 proxy is given names to look up itself, so not even those lookups leave the Mac.
- **The built-in Tor.** The Tor Project's own tor, from its expert bundle for apps that include Tor, sits in `Contents/Helpers` with its libevent in `Contents/Frameworks`, signed to run inside PMail's sandbox. PMail starts it with its whole configuration on the command line: a client only, a SOCKS port on 127.0.0.1 (a free one each time), a circuit of its own for each server, its data in PMail's folder, and PMail as its owner, so it quits if PMail does. PMail follows its progress from what it prints, holds requests until it's connected, then sends them through it as through any SOCKS5 proxy, and starts it again a few times if it stops.
- **Descriptions.** A formatted description is kept as the simple HTML Google's calendar uses (bold, italic, underline, links, lists, and `<br>` between lines) beside its plain text, which searching and meeting links use. Google keeps it as the description, Microsoft as an HTML body, and iCalendar files as X-ALT-DESC next to DESCRIPTION. HTML from providers is cut down to those tags before PMail shows it, so nothing in a description loads from the internet.
- **Places.** Location suggestions come from MapKit's search completer (addresses and points of interest), which needs no location access; the chosen place's name and address go in the event's location, as text every calendar keeps. Sample runs use made-up places and never ask Apple Maps.
- **Repeating, and who's free.** The editor works with a repeat rule (frequency, interval, weekdays, a weekday of the month, and an end) read from and written as an RRULE line, keeping dates taken out one at a time (EXDATE). Microsoft calendars describe rules their own way, so a series' rule is read from its series master (once per series) and written as Graph's pattern and range, starting where the series starts; repeating Microsoft events are written in the Mac's time zone so their time of day holds across daylight saving changes. Busy times come from Google's free/busy lookup and Microsoft's schedules; your own come from your calendars.
- **Tasks and kinds of events.** Each account's calendar service is wrapped with its task lists, which show as calendars of their own (`tasks:` and the list's ID) whose events are all-day tasks; changes to a task go to the task service (Google Tasks, Microsoft To Do, or a JSON file in the account's folder). A Google event's kind is its event type, which can't be changed once made, so changing it makes the event again and deletes the old one; a calendar that refuses out of office or focus time is remembered and gets ordinary events from then on. Microsoft's out of office is the event's "show as". When a calendar saves an event as another kind than the one chosen, the kind is kept in the account's folder by calendar and event (a repeating event's by its series) and put back on the event whenever it loads. Google keeps a task's due date as midnight UTC on that date, and To Do tasks are written at noon UTC so every app reads the same date.
- **Meetings.** Google events get Meet through the calendar API's conference request, and Microsoft events get Teams by asking the calendar for an online meeting; PMail waits for the link if the provider is still making it. A FaceTime link is saved in the event's URL and description, so every calendar app shows it. Meeting links in any event's location or description are recognized for its Join button.
- **Keeping up to date.** Every couple of minutes each account's service is asked what's new in its inbox since the last time, cheaply: Google's mail history, Microsoft's newest inbox messages, an IMAP inbox's next message number, a POP server's message list. The IMAP account showing also keeps a second connection open on its inbox, waiting with IDLE where the server has it (renewed every 25 minutes) and asking every minute where it doesn't, and checks the moment something changes. What's new is announced with the system's notifications and the window refreshes what it shows.
- **The copy on the Mac.** Each account has a folder of its own with a copy of its folders, the pages of conversations it listed, the conversations it opened (minus big attachments' data) and its calendars, events and contacts; the window shows the copy first and the server's answer replaces it. Snoozes and the Outbox (and Scheduled) are kept there too, one file per waiting message, so nothing is lost if PMail quits, as are drafts saved offline and calendar and contact changes made offline, each list in a file of its own, oldest first. Changes to the same thing are merged as they're kept (one made and then deleted offline is never sent), shown on top of what each load brings, and sent in order beside each mail check, a new event's or contact's later changes going to it by the ID the server gives it; whatever the server turns down for a reason other than being unreachable is dropped and reported. Snoozed conversations are found again by their Message-IDs when they come back, since archiving can give a message a new ID.
- **Folders kept on the Mac.** An IMAP account's folders come from the plain LIST, with special-use marks (RFC 6154) asked for too where the server offers them, then the usual names (Sent Messages, Deleted Messages, Junk…). Sent, Drafts, Archive, Junk and Trash the server still lacks are served from a store on the Mac, the same kind a POP account keeps its mail in, beside the server's folders. Deleting reads the whole message and stores it before deleting it from the server; moving a kept message to a server folder uploads it with APPEND, then lets the copy on the Mac go.
- **Inbox categories.** For servers that don't sort mail, each inbox message is sorted once from its envelope and four headers (List-Unsubscribe, List-Id, Precedence, Auto-Submitted), newest first and in batches, by rules that only file mail away from Primary when it shows signs of being automated (list headers, no-reply and role addresses, social networks' domains), then by its subject. The sorting is kept in memory with each message's sender, so moving a sender to another category applies at once, and a tab's unread count is one more search for unread mail.
- **Labels.** IMAP labels are keywords named `PMail_` and the label's name in letters a keyword can hold; a mailbox that doesn't take keywords has its labels kept on the Mac by Message-ID instead, and listing a label searches both. PMail also remembers where each labeled message was last seen (its mailbox and UID) and fetches those directly when a label is opened, keeping the ones that still have it, so a label lists its messages even where a server's own searches don't find them. Google labels and Microsoft categories go through their own APIs.
- **Blocked senders and junk.** Each account keeps a list of blocked addresses and domains on the Mac. Every check for new mail deals with messages from them first (moved through the account's own Report spam, the server's or the one kept on the Mac; moved to the Trash; or marked), then judges the rest for junk before announcing what's left. The junk filter is a naive Bayes filter: each word's chance of meaning junk comes from how often it was in junk and good mail, pulled towards even while it's rare, and a message's 150 most telling words are combined with Fisher's method into a score from 0 to 1; 0.9 or more is junk. Words come from the sender's address, name and domain, the subject, the first lines and signs like an unsubscribe header, each tagged with where it came from. IMAP summaries also fetch X-Spam-Flag, X-Spam-Status, X-Spam and Exchange's spam confidence level, for servers' own junk marks. Marks are kept by Message-ID, so they follow a message wherever it's listed; the people written to are recorded as each message is sent, and from the Sent folder when an account starts.
- **The day and week views** share a day between events that overlap: each takes the first free column at its start and stretches right where nothing overlaps it.
